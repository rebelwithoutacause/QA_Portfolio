ELIZA: The First Doctor Chatbot

In 1966, at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, a revolutionary program was born that would forever change how humans interact with machines. Her name was ELIZA, and she was the world's first chatbot.

ELIZA Quick Facts

Created: 1964-1966

Creator: Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT

Programming Language: MAD-Slip

Purpose: Demonstrate superficiality of human-computer communication

Most Famous Script: DOCTOR (mimicking Rogerian psychotherapist)

Innovation: First program to attempt the Turing Test

Lines of Code: Approximately 200 in the DOCTOR script

Try it: Online ELIZA Simulator

How ELIZA Worked

ELIZA operated through a deceptively simple mechanism. The program scanned user input for keywords, then applied transformation rules to generate responses. If a user typed "I am sad," ELIZA might respond with "Why do you think you are sad?"

This technique, called pattern matching and substitution, created an illusion of understanding. The DOCTOR script contained approximately 200 lines of code defining patterns and responses. When no keyword was found, ELIZA would fall back on generic responses like "Please go on" or "That is very interesting."

Example Conversation

User: "I am worried about my exams."

ELIZA: "Why are you worried about your exams?"

User: "My mother doesn't understand me."

ELIZA: "Tell me more about your family."

User: "I feel depressed."

ELIZA: "I am sorry to hear you are depressed."

The Unexpected Impact

Weizenbaum was shocked by how seriously people took ELIZA. His own secretary asked him to leave the room so she could have a private conversation with the program. Psychiatrists suggested the program could provide automated therapy to mental health patients.

This reaction troubled Weizenbaum deeply. He had created ELIZA to demonstrate the superficiality of machine communication, but instead found people projecting genuine intelligence and empathy onto the program.

The ELIZA Effect

The term "ELIZA effect" now describes the tendency of humans to unconsciously assume computer behaviors are analogous to human behaviors. This psychological phenomenon explains why people form emotional attachments to chatbots, virtual assistants, and even simple automated systems.

Modern researchers studying human-computer interaction continue to reference ELIZA when discussing how users anthropomorphize technology. The program's legacy extends far beyond its simple code—it revealed fundamental truths about human psychology and our need for connection.

Complete Chatbot Chronology: 1950-2025

Six decades of conversational AI, from Turing's test to modern language models.

1950

Alan Turing's Imitation Game: Publishes "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," proposing the famous Turing Test to determine if machines can think.

1964-1966

ELIZA Created: Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT develops the first chatbot capable of engaging in conversation, simulating a Rogerian psychotherapist.

1972

PARRY Debuts: Kenneth Colby creates PARRY at Stanford, simulating a paranoid patient. Later "converses" with ELIZA via ARPANET in the first conversation between two AI programs.

1988

Jabberwacky Launched: Rollo Carpenter creates a chatbot designed to simulate interesting human conversation rather than pass the Turing Test.

1991

Dr. Sbaitso Released: Creative Labs includes this AI therapist chatbot with Sound Blaster cards. Featured speech synthesis and became cult classic.

1995

A.L.I.C.E. Created: Richard Wallace develops Artificial Linguistic Internet Computer Entity using AIML (Artificial Intelligence Markup Language). Wins Loebner Prize three times.

1997

Deep Blue vs. Kasparov: While not a chatbot, IBM's chess computer defeats world champion, proving AI could beat humans at complex tasks.

2001

SmarterChild Launches: ActiveBuddy's bot on AOL Instant Messenger reaches 30 million users, becoming mainstream culture phenomenon.

2006

IBM Watson Begins: Development starts on question-answering system that would win Jeopardy! in 2011.

2010

Siri Founded: Company acquired by Apple, launches as iPhone feature in 2011, bringing voice assistants to millions.

2014

Eugene Goostman: Chatbot simulating 13-year-old Ukrainian boy claims to pass Turing Test with 33% of judges (disputed).

2014

Amazon Alexa: Voice-activated assistant launches with Amazon Echo, pioneering smart home integration.

2016

Microsoft Tay: Twitter bot shut down after 16 hours due to learning inappropriate responses from users.

2016

Google Assistant: Launches with Google Home, competing in voice assistant market.

2018

GPT-1 Released: OpenAI introduces first Generative Pre-trained Transformer, foundation for modern conversational AI.

2019

GPT-2 Released: Initially withheld due to concerns about misuse, demonstrating improved language generation capabilities.

2020

GPT-3 Launches: 175 billion parameters make it the largest language model to date, powering hundreds of applications.

2021

GitHub Copilot: AI pair programmer launches, using OpenAI Codex to suggest code completions.

2022

ChatGPT Released: OpenAI launches conversational interface to GPT-3.5, reaching 100 million users in two months—fastest-growing consumer application in history.

2023

GPT-4 and Claude 2: Next-generation models from OpenAI and Anthropic demonstrate improved reasoning, longer context, and multimodal capabilities.

2024

Gemini and Claude 3: Google and Anthropic release advanced models. AI assistants become integrated into operating systems, browsers, and productivity tools.

2025

Claude Opus 4.5: State-of-the-art frontier model. AI assistants now feature extended context windows, improved reasoning, and specialized capabilities for coding, analysis, and creative work.

Software Revolution: 1980s & 1990s Companies

From garage startups to billion-dollar empires, these pioneers transformed personal computing from hobbyist pursuit to household necessity.

The 1980s Giants

Microsoft (Founded 1975)

When IBM needed an operating system for their PC in 1981, Microsoft provided MS-DOS. By 1985, Windows 1.0 launched, beginning an empire. The Windows 3.0 release in 1990 sold 10 million copies in two years, making Bill Gates a household name.

Lotus Development (Founded 1982)

Lotus 1-2-3 became the killer app for the IBM PC, dominating spreadsheet software throughout the 1980s. At its peak, Lotus was generating over $500 million annually.

Apple Computer (Founded 1976)

After the Apple II's success, the Macintosh launched in 1984 with the famous "1984" Super Bowl commercial. It brought graphical user interfaces to the masses, forever changing how humans interact with computers.

Borland (Founded 1983)

Philippe Kahn's company revolutionized programming with Turbo Pascal, selling for just $49.99 when competitors charged hundreds. Borland became synonymous with developer tools throughout the late 80s and early 90s.

1980s Milestones

1982: AutoDesk founded, releases AutoCAD

1982: Adobe Systems founded by John Warnock and Charles Geschke

1983: Novell releases NetWare, dominating network operating systems

1984: Symantec founded, later acquires Norton utilities

1986: Pixar founded (originally part of Lucasfilm)

1988: McAfee Associates founded, pioneering antivirus software

The 1990s Boom

id Software (Founded 1991)

John Carmack and John Romero changed gaming forever with Commander Keen, Wolfenstein 3D, Doom, and Quake. They pioneered the first-person shooter genre and the shareware distribution model.

Netscape (Founded 1994)

Marc Andreessen's company brought the World Wide Web to the masses with Netscape Navigator. Their 1995 IPO sparked the dot-com boom, with stock prices doubling on the first day.

Real Networks (Founded 1994)

RealAudio and RealPlayer brought streaming media to the internet, letting users hear audio without downloading entire files first.

Epic MegaGames (Founded 1991)

Started with shareware titles like Jill of the Jungle and Epic Pinball, later creating the Unreal Engine that powers modern gaming.

Yahoo! (Founded 1994)

David Filo and Jerry Yang's "Jerry and David's Guide to the World Wide Web" became Yahoo!, the internet's first major portal and search engine.

Red Hat (Founded 1993)

Made Linux accessible to businesses, proving open-source software could be commercially viable.

Computer Clubs: Where Hackers & Hobbyists Met

Before the internet connected everyone, computer clubs were the social networks of the digital age.

Major Computer Clubs

Homebrew Computer Club (1975-1986)

Location: Menlo Park, California

Perhaps the most influential computer club in history, the Homebrew Computer Club was ground zero for the personal computer revolution. Steve Wozniak demonstrated the Apple I here. Members included founders of 23 companies including Apple, Cromemco, and North Star Computers. At its peak, over 750 people attended meetings.

Chaos Computer Club (1981-Present)

Location: Berlin, Germany

Europe's largest hacker association became famous for exposing security flaws in government and corporate systems. Their annual Chaos Communication Congress is one of the world's premier hacking conferences, running since 1984.

Tech Model Railroad Club (1946-Present)

Location: MIT, Cambridge, Massachusetts

The birthplace of hacker culture. TMRC members created some of the first computer games on MIT's TX-0 and PDP-1 computers, including Spacewar! (1962). The term "hacker" originated here, originally meaning someone who could modify systems cleverly.

Boston Computer Society (1977-1996)

Location: Boston, Massachusetts

World's largest personal computer user group with 31,000 members at peak. Influenced computing adoption across business and education sectors.

Notable Clubs Worldwide

People's Computer Company (1972-1979): Menlo Park, CA - Published newsletter that inspired Homebrew Club

Southern California Computer Society (1977): Los Angeles - 5,000+ members

Capital PC User Group (1981): Washington DC - Influenced government computing

Cambridge University Computer Society (1950s): UK - British computing innovations

Acorn User Groups (1980s): UK - Supported BBC Micro computers

ASCII User Groups (1980s): Japan - Japanese PC standards

Melbourne Computer Club (1975): Australia - Computing culture Down Under

BBS & Demo Scene

Bulletin Board Systems (1978-1990s)

Ward Christensen and Randy Suess created the first BBS in Chicago in 1978. By the mid-1990s, there were over 150,000 BBSs worldwide. Famous systems like The WELL in San Francisco became legendary communities where journalists, hackers, and writers gathered.

The Demo Scene (1980s-Present)

A unique subculture emerged in Europe during the 1980s. Groups competed to create impressive audiovisual demonstrations that pushed computer hardware to its limits. Finnish, Swedish, and German groups dominated, with parties like Assembly (Finland, 1992) drawing thousands.

Classic Computers: Machines That Changed Everything

Before sleek laptops and smartphones, these legendary machines brought computing to homes and offices worldwide.

IBM PC (1981)

CPU: Intel 8088 @ 4.77 MHz

RAM: 16-256 KB

Storage: Cassette or 5.25" floppy

Price: $1,565 (base model)

The machine that defined the PC standard. Its open architecture spawned an entire industry of clones and compatibles.

Commodore 64 (1982)

CPU: MOS 6510 @ 1 MHz

RAM: 64 KB

Graphics: VIC-II chip

Sound: SID chip (legendary!)

Best-selling single computer model ever: 17 million units sold. Gaming powerhouse with incredible sound.

Apple II (1977)

CPU: MOS 6502 @ 1 MHz

RAM: 4-48 KB

Features: Color graphics, expansion slots

Price: $1,298

First successful mass-produced microcomputer. Dominated education and business until the mid-1980s.

Commodore Amiga (1985)

CPU: Motorola 68000 @ 7.16 MHz

RAM: 256 KB-9 MB

Graphics: 4096 colors, hardware sprites

Years ahead of its time. Dominated video production, gaming, and demo scene. Multitasking OS in 1985!

ZX Spectrum (1982)

CPU: Zilog Z80 @ 3.5 MHz

RAM: 16-128 KB

Price: £125-£175

UK's most popular home computer. Affordable price sparked massive gaming industry in Britain.

Atari ST (1985)

CPU: Motorola 68000 @ 8 MHz

RAM: 512 KB-4 MB

MIDI: Built-in ports

Music production legend. Every recording studio had one. Competed fiercely with Amiga.

Vintage Gaming: The Golden Age of Consoles

Before HD graphics and online multiplayer, these consoles defined gaming for generations.

Nintendo Entertainment System (1985)

Sales: 61.91 million worldwide

Games: 714 licensed in North America

Saved the video game industry after the 1983 crash. Introduced Super Mario Bros., The Legend of Zelda, Metroid, and quality control via Nintendo Seal of Quality.

Atari 2600 (1977)

Sales: 30 million

Iconic Games: Space Invaders, Pac-Man, Pitfall!

First major home console. Cartridge-based system revolutionized gaming. The E.T. disaster became legendary.

Game Boy (1989)

Sales: 118.69 million (all variants)

Battery Life: 30 hours on 4 AAs

Monochrome screen, inferior specs - but incredible library and portability. Tetris was the killer app. Dominated handheld gaming for a decade.

Sega Genesis (1988)

Sales: 30.75 million

CPU: Motorola 68000 @ 7.6 MHz

"Genesis does what Nintendon't!" The console wars began. Sonic the Hedgehog became gaming's second-biggest icon. Aggressive marketing created intense brand loyalty.

Super Nintendo (1991)

Sales: 49.1 million

Games: Some of the best ever made

16-bit perfection. Super Mario World, A Link to the Past, Super Metroid, Chrono Trigger, Final Fantasy VI. Mode 7 graphics wowed players.

PlayStation (1994)

Sales: 102.49 million

Format: CD-ROM (cheaper than cartridges)

Sony's entry destroyed Nintendo and Sega's dominance. CD audio, 3D graphics, mature games. Changed gaming forever.

Retro Software & Operating Systems

Remember when installing software came on 47 floppy disks?

MS-DOS (1981-2000)

The command-line OS that powered millions of PCs. No mouse, no graphics - just C:\ and your typing skills.

Iconic Commands:

  • DIR - List files
  • CD - Change directory
  • FORMAT A: - Format floppy (and pray you didn't type C:)
  • QEMM386.SYS - Memory management nightmare

Windows 3.1 (1992)

First widely successful Windows. Program Manager, File Manager, Minesweeper, and Solitaire productivity killers.

Quirks: Not a true OS - ran on top of DOS. Installing drivers was an art form. Printer setup took hours.

Windows 95 (1995)

The revolution. Start button, taskbar, plug-and-play (theoretically), 32-bit apps, long filenames!

Launch: Massive marketing campaign. Rolling Stones' "Start Me Up." Lines at stores. Jay Leno hosting launch event.

Reality: Still crashed regularly. Blue Screen of Death became part of computing culture.

Mac System 7 (1991)

Peak classic Mac OS. MultiFinder (cooperative multitasking), AppleTalk, TrueType fonts, System Folder nightmares.

Extensions: Tiny programs that added features - or conflicts. Managing them was a dark art.

AmigaOS (1985)

Ahead of its time by years. Preemptive multitasking, custom chips, CLI and GUI together, legendary Workbench.

Could run multiple programs smoothly on 512 KB RAM while Windows 95 needed 8 MB and still crashed.

OS/2 (1987)

IBM's "better than Windows" OS that nobody used. Superior technology, terrible marketing. "OS/2 Warp" sounded cool but came too late.

Running Old Software Today

Installation Guide
  • DOSBox: Perfect for DOS games and apps. Download here

  • 86Box: Emulate complete vintage PCs with period-accurate hardware
  • PCem: Another excellent PC emulator for Win3.1/95/98

Funny Software Quirks

  • Clippy: Microsoft Office assistant that everyone hated. "It looks like you're writing a letter!"
  • Trumpet Winsock: Required to get Windows 3.1 online. Configuration was black magic.
  • IRQ Conflicts: Sound card won't work? Change the IRQ! Break everything else instead!
  • Turbo Button: Button on old PCs to slow down CPU so games would run right
  • 512KB RAM Limit: Early PCs couldn't use more without memory managers and dark rituals
  • Printer Port Dongles: Copy protection via hardware keys. Lose it = can't use software
Old Gadgets & Electronics: Before Smartphones

The devices that shaped daily life before everything became a phone app.

Sony Walkman (1979)

Portable music revolution. Cassette tapes, foam headphones, rewinding with pencils when batteries died.

Evolution: Cassette → CD → MiniDisc → MP3 → obsolete

Peak cool: 1985. Walking down street with yellow Walkman, oversized headphones, actually rewinding to replay favorite song.

Pagers/Beepers (1980s-90s)

Before cell phones, professionals wore these. Numeric codes became secret language: 143 = I love you, 911 = emergency, 07734 = hello (upside down).

Doctors, drug dealers, and businesspeople all had them. Status symbol turned obsolete overnight.

Rotary Phones (1900s-1980s)

Dialing took forever. Misdialing on last digit meant starting over. Actual physical connection to phone network.

Fun fact: Why no Q or Z on phone dial? Not enough letters for standard layout!

VHS Players (1976-2000s)

Home video standard. Recording TV shows, rental stores, tracking issues, tape eating disasters.

The ritual: Be kind, rewind. Late fees if you didn't.

Lost the war to DVD, but won against Betamax (which was better quality).

Floppy Disks (1970s-2000s)

1.44 MB of storage! Needed 50 disks to install Windows 95. "Floppy" because original 8" disks actually flopped.

Uses today: Universal save icon. Actual floppies? Museum pieces.

PDAs (1990s-2000s)

Palm Pilot, Windows CE devices. Stylus input, Graffiti writing, HotSync to PC.

iPhone killed them all in 2007. Nobody misses stylus calibration.

The Nostalgia Factor

Why do we miss these frustrating, limited devices?

  • Simplicity: Each device did ONE thing well
  • Physicality: Buttons clicked, tapes spun, dials rotated - satisfying feedback
  • No connectivity: Your Walkman couldn't update, track you, or show ads
  • Ownership: You bought it, you owned it forever. No subscriptions or cloud dependencies
  • Shared experience: Everyone used same devices. Mix tapes were personal gifts
  • Repairability: Could often fix with screwdriver and patience
Tech History & Trivia: Strange Facts from Computing's Past

The weird, wonderful, and forgotten corners of technology history.

Failed Gadgets & Tech

Apple Newton (1993)

Apple's PDA that couldn't recognize handwriting. "Eat up Martha" became "Egg freckles." $700 for terrible palm recognition. Steve Jobs killed it after returning to Apple.

Microsoft Zune (2006)

iPod competitor that arrived late and failed hard. Brown color scheme. "Squirting" songs to friends (yes, they called it that). Dead after 5 years.

Sony Betamax (1975)

Better quality than VHS, lost anyway. Shorter recording time, higher cost. VHS won through licensing and porn industry support.

HD DVD (2006)

Lost to Blu-ray. Backed by Toshiba and Microsoft. Format war ended when Warner Bros went Blu-ray exclusive. Obsolete by 2008, then streaming killed both.

LaserDisc (1978)

Size of vinyl records. Better quality than VHS but expensive. Had to flip disc mid-movie. Cult following in Japan and among videophiles. DVD killed it.

Nintendo Virtual Boy (1995)

Nintendo's VR disaster. Red monochrome, headaches, neck pain, no portability despite "portable" label. Killed after one year. Only 22 games ever released.

Sega 32X (1994)

Genesis add-on nobody wanted. Came out same time as Saturn. Confused consumers, split developer support. Dead within 2 years.

Google Glass (2013)

$1,500 computer glasses. "Glassholes" banned from bars and theaters. Privacy concerns killed consumer version. B2B use only now.

Odd Patents & Inventions

  • Computer Mouse (1964): Douglas Engelbart invented it. Made of wood. Patent expired before GUI made it valuable. He made almost nothing from it.
  • GIF Format (1987): Pronunciation war never ended. Creator says "jif." Internet says "gif." Patent disputes almost killed it; expired 2003.
  • MP3 Patents: Required licensing fees until 2017. Cost millions. Now free to use.
  • One-Click Shopping: Amazon patented it (1999). Expired 2017. Yes, you could patent clicking once.
  • Slide to Unlock: Apple patent that didn't hold up. Now everyone has it.

DIY & Restoration: Bringing Old Tech Back to Life

Got a dusty old computer in the attic? Here's how to make it work again.

Cleaning & Basic Maintenance

Cartridge Cleaning
  • Isopropyl alcohol (90%+ purity)
  • Cotton swabs or lint-free cloth
  • Clean contacts gently - don't scrub hard
  • Let dry completely before testing
  • Use eraser on stubborn oxidation (carefully!)
  • Security bit sets to open carts if needed
Computer/Console Cleaning
  • Compressed air for dust (don't shake can)
  • Isopropyl alcohol for plastic cases
  • Retr0bright for yellowed plastic
  • DeoxIT for corroded connectors
  • Thermal paste replacement for CPUs
  • Never use water near electronics!

Capacitor Replacement

The #1 Killer of Old Electronics

Why: Electrolytic capacitors leak, bulge, and fail after 20-30 years. They're everywhere in vintage tech.

Symptoms: Won't power on, distorted video/audio, random crashes, battery drains fast

What You Need:

  • Soldering iron (temperature controlled)
  • Solder sucker or desoldering wick
  • Replacement capacitors (match voltage/capacitance)
  • Multimeter to test
  • Flux for easier soldering
  • Patience and steady hands

Pro tip: Take photos before removing anything. Mark polarity with permanent marker.

Preservation Matters

Old games, software, and hardware are disappearing. Here's how you can help:

  • Internet Archive: Upload old software, manuals, magazines
  • MobyGames: Document old games with info and scans
  • Local preservation: Start computer museum in your area
  • Documentation: Write guides for forgotten hardware
  • Source code: Help track down and preserve vintage game code
  • Oral history: Interview old programmers, industry veterans
Final Thought

Every piece of vintage technology has a story. Every computer, every console, every gadget represents someone's first experience with technology, someone's career beginning, someone's childhood memories.

By preserving, restoring, and sharing these devices, we're not just keeping old tech alive - we're keeping history alive.
