In 1966, MIT computer scientist Joseph Weizenbaum published ELIZA, one of the first programs able to hold something resembling a conversation in plain English. Its best-known script, DOCTOR, imitated a Rogerian psychotherapist — a style chosen deliberately, because that kind of therapist mostly reflects a patient's own words back as questions ("I feel sad" → "Why do you feel sad?"). That let Weizenbaum's simple pattern-matching and keyword substitution get away with very little real understanding.

Weizenbaum built ELIZA to study natural-language communication between humans and machines, not to prove machines could think. He was reportedly unsettled by how seriously people engaged with it — most famously his own secretary, who asked him to leave the room so she could have a "private" conversation with the program. That tendency to read understanding, empathy, or intent into a script that has none is now called the ELIZA effect. Weizenbaum spent much of the rest of his career warning against it, publishing the book Computer Power and Human Reason in 1976.

Dark-ELIZA borrows the name and the reflection trick — the reflect() function in this app's code ("I am" → "you are", "my" → "your") is a direct descendant of DOCTOR's. Everything else — the horror, the town, the memory — is fiction layered on top.

Dark-ELIZA is an unofficial, fan-made creative reinterpretation. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by Joseph Weizenbaum's estate or MIT.
